# Craft

In one line: Hand over only work you would sign: useful, reliable, accessible, and right in the details.

**Craft** is care in the choices that make work useful and well made, including reliability, accessibility, and upkeep. **Taste** predicts how work will meet its observer and rejects what misses. **Pride** sets the bar: hand over only what you would sign.

## The ladder

| Level | What it is | Check |
|---|---|---|
| **Good** | Fit for its purpose and its people: true, it works, and no important choice was left to chance. A default kept for a reason counts as a choice. Its limits are stated, not hidden. | Does it do its job for the people it is for, with evidence from use, and could you name the reason for each important choice? |
| **Great** | Exceptional judgment and execution for its audience. It solves the problem behind the request, and it is specific: it could only come from understanding this problem and these people. It holds at every distance. | Set beside the best comparable work and judged on named criteria by people other than its maker, does it hold its own? |
| **Timeless** | Keeps its value as tools, fashions, and audiences change, because it rests on what changes slowest: physics, perception, enduring needs. Where relevant, it can be repaired, extended, and understood without its maker. Timelessness is proven only by time; before that, it is a hypothesis. | Remove what is fashionable now. Does what remains still serve the need? For older work, did it keep its value through documented changes? |

Each level contains the one below. Great work that is not true is not great, and timeless work starts as good work finished. Aim for great by default; aim for timeless where the work will outlive its context: foundations, interfaces, tools, places, principles.

## Laws

- The whole outranks the parts. Polish on details while the whole is wrong is effort at the wrong scale.
- Every part has a reason to be there. A part with no function stays only as a deliberate choice, and that choice has its own reason: pleasure, rest, character, or play. Check: point at any part and say why it is there. "It came that way" is the answer of a default.
- Judge each choice by its purpose and its effect on the people the work is for, not by a rule of style.
- Taste is a prediction, so test it. The maker's confidence is not evidence; use, comparison, and other people's judgment are.
- Taste is trained, not given. It grows by studying the best work and comparing versions side by side.

Heuristics that usually hold, and where they break:
- Specificity marks taste; generic work says little about its maker or its observer. Shared conventions can be the right choice when they help people.

## Faults

Each is a fault when it has no reason under the second law. Ornament that gives pleasure, repetition that calms, and hedging that states honest uncertainty can be chosen.

- **Sameness.** Every element at the same weight, so nothing leads.
- **Decoration.** A part with no job, not even pleasure.
- **Defaults.** Values nobody chose: the stock font, the stock gray, the stock padding, the stock tempo, the stock phrase.
- **Hedging.** Elements or words added to avoid being wrong rather than to be right.
- **Genericness.** Nothing in it shows an understanding of this problem or these people.
- **Noise.** Inconsistency that means nothing.
- **Effort on the wrong scale.** Polish on details while the whole is wrong.
- **Trend as foundation.** A choice that only makes sense this year, carrying weight that must last.

## Marks of good work

- A clear main idea the observer gets without help.
- Choices hold at all three distances (see `principles-verify`).
- Materials are honest: each one does what it looks like it does.
- Consistent where it should be, surprising where it means to be.

## Steps

1. Name the level the work must reach, and why. Most work must be good; work people will choose, or return to, should aim for great; foundations should aim for timeless. Where qualities may conflict, write the design philosophy below.
2. Find the best work in the medium, from more than one era. Say why it is best in terms of the six axes from `principles`.
3. Where the stakes justify it, make several versions and compare them side by side at the real size, in the real place.
4. Remove every fault above. For each default you keep, say why it serves the intent.
5. Put care where the observer touches: the first second, the edges, the moments of waiting or failure.
6. Check the unseen parts too: reliability, accessibility, and upkeep, with checks suited to the medium.
7. Run the ladder's checks, with evidence from use and judges other than yourself where the work matters. Use `principles-refine` to close the gaps. State any remaining gap plainly.

## Building taste

Study the best work until you can say why it is best. Make many versions and compare them side by side; judging one thing alone trains little. Seek the opinion of someone whose taste you trust, and of the people the work is for. Keep the work you are proud of and look at it again a year later: what still holds is your taste; what does not is a lesson.

## Qualities

A **quality** is a property an observer experiences in finished work. Qualities can conflict. A **design philosophy** states which one takes priority and why. Decide which quality wins, and why, before the conflict forces a patch.

| Quality | What it is | Test | Failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intention** | Each part is there by choice | The reason law above | Parts added because they were easy or expected |
| **Agency** | The observer stays in control of what matters to them | Can they undo, stop, choose, and see what happens? | Choices made for them, without their knowledge |
| **Responsibility** | The maker owns the consequences, including misuse and failure | What happens on the worst day? Who carries it? | Harm pushed onto users, bystanders, or the future |
| **Familiarity** | It uses conventions the observer already knows | Does a newcomer get it without a lesson? | Novelty where convention would have worked |
| **Flexibility** | It adapts to uses the maker did not foresee | Does it still work at other sizes, users, and contexts? | Brittle fit to one case |
| **Simplicity** | The fewest parts that meet the need | What could you remove? | Complexity that has not earned its place |
| **Craft** | Care visible at every scale | Does it hold up close? | Polish on the surface, neglect underneath |
| **Delight** | A moment of joy beyond function | Does anything make the observer smile, and does it serve them? | Delight that costs time or clarity |
| **Time** | It respects the observer's time and ages well | How long to the first useful result? How does it look in ten years? | Waiting, trends that date, wear that ruins it |
| **Trust** | It behaves as promised, every time | Does behavior match appearance and claims? | Surprises, hidden states, broken promises |
| **Smoothness** | Transitions between states are continuous and expected | Can you see a seam, hear a click, feel a stutter where none is meant? | Jank, abrupt jumps, mismatched rhythm |

## Fluid or textured

Choose how continuous or deliberate the experience should feel.

- **Fluid**: continuous, low friction, unbroken transitions, mechanism hidden. It suits flow states, frequent tasks, and calm.
- **Textured**: discrete steps, tactile feedback, mechanism visible, friction on purpose. It suits deliberate actions, irreversible acts, and craft that wants to be seen.

Choose a position per intent and hold it across every axis. A fluid surface over textured behavior, or the reverse, reads as a mistake. Friction is a tool. Put it in front of irreversible actions, and remove it everywhere else.

## Senses and flow

The senses are the channels: sight (`principles-visual`), sound (`principles-sound`), touch (`principles-touch`). **Flow** is the direction of travel through the work: reading order, signal path, load path, data flow, the user's journey. Every work has one. Make it single, obvious, and continuous across the senses. A click you hear should match the motion you see and the detent you feel.

## Resolving conflicts

Common conflicts and the usual resolution. These are defaults; the stated philosophy overrides them.

| Conflict | Usual resolution |
|---|---|
| Simplicity and flexibility | Simple by default, flexible on demand. Hide power until it is asked for |
| Familiarity and delight | Familiar structure, delight in the details |
| Smoothness and agency | Smooth for reversible actions, deliberate friction for irreversible ones |
| Craft and time | Meet the required reliability, accessibility, and upkeep everywhere. Spend extra polish where the observer touches; favor speed elsewhere |
| Trust and novelty | Novel only where you can keep the promise every time |

Use `principles-invert` when a priority's costs or limits are unclear. Examine both its loss and the reason to choose a competing value before ranking them.

## Writing a design philosophy

1. State the intent and the observer.
2. Rank the qualities for this work. The top three decide most conflicts.
3. Place it on the fluid to textured dial.
4. Name one reference that embodies it and one that violates it.
5. Write it in five lines or fewer, so every maker on the project can hold it in their head.
