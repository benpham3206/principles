# Design

**Design** chooses how a made thing will serve a need within its constraints. Its result is a coherent set of choices that can be made and checked. Novelty is useful when it serves the need; a known solution can be the right design.

Use `principles-method` to set the scope and rigor. Use the matching medium skills to make the choices concrete, and `principles-refine` to revise an existing result.

## Steps

1. Name the people, their situation, and the need. Separate evidence of the need from assumptions and from the proposed solution. State the outcome and the experience the work should give them.
2. Name the constraints before choosing a form or mechanism. Rank the qualities that may trade off with `principles-qualities`; a preference cannot compensate for a broken hard constraint.
3. Find the decisions that could change the approach. Use `principles-represent` if the problem's structure hides them. Compare meaningfully different approaches where uncertainty warrants it, including an existing solution. Skip extra concepts when the evidence already fixes the choice. Use `principles-synthesize` when a new approach is needed.
4. Give each candidate a main idea. Choose across the six axes from `principles`, using the medium skills. Make the parts support that idea: form, behavior, construction, and upkeep must fit together. Include access, failure, recovery, and change over time where they matter.
5. Make the cheapest prototype that can expose the most consequential uncertainty. A sketch can test arrangement; it cannot prove timing, physical grip, or structural strength. Match the prototype's fidelity to the question, then try it in the conditions that matter.
6. Compare the candidates against the same need, constraints, and named criteria. Show the trade-offs and the evidence; use numbers where they mean something and comparative judgment where they do not. Choose a coherent whole rather than combining incompatible favorite parts. If none serves the need, reopen the framing or constraints with their owner.
7. Leave enough to build and check the choice: a model, sketch, sample, or prototype; the reason for the consequential decisions; and unresolved assumptions with their next tests. Settle the interfaces with `principles-integrate`. Carry the chosen intent and criteria into implementation and refinement.

## Principles

These are heuristics. Scale exploration and documentation to what a wrong choice would cost.

| Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Design for the actual situation | An elegant answer to a need nobody has | What evidence shows who needs it, where they meet it, and what they must do? |
| Let constraints shape the approach | An attractive concept patched late to fit reality | Does the candidate meet every hard constraint before its preferences are compared? |
| Explore choices that could change the answer | One early idea defended by cosmetic variants, or needless alternatives after the answer is clear | Which unresolved decision could favor a different approach, and what would settle it? |
| Give the whole one main idea | Parts that compete for attention or require conflicting behavior | Can each consequential choice be traced to the intent, and do the parts work together in use? |
| Test the property the decision depends on | A persuasive picture taken as proof of use or construction | Could this prototype fail on the property being claimed, under the relevant conditions? |
| Preserve the reasons for the choice | Later changes undo a trade-off nobody remembers | Can another maker tell what is fixed, what is provisional, and why? |

Pairs with: before `principles-method`, `principles-qualities`, `principles-represent`; after `principles-synthesize`, `principles-integrate`, `principles-refine`, `principles-verify`.
