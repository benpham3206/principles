# Design

In one line: Name the need and constraints first, then test the one uncertainty that could change the approach.

**Design** chooses how something will serve a need within its constraints. Leave a set of choices concrete enough to build and check. Use a new approach when it serves the need; a known solution can be the right design.

Use `principles-method` to set the scope and rigor. Use the matching medium skills to make the choices concrete, and `principles-refine` to revise an existing result.

## Steps

1. Name the people, their situation, and the need. Separate evidence of the need from assumptions and from the proposed solution. State the outcome and the experience the work should give them.
2. Name the constraints before choosing a form or mechanism. Rank the qualities that may trade off with `principles-craft`; a preference cannot compensate for a broken hard constraint.
3. Find the decisions that could change the approach. Use `principles-represent` if the problem's structure hides them. Compare meaningfully different approaches where uncertainty warrants it, including an existing solution. Skip extra concepts when the evidence already fixes the choice. Use `principles-synthesize` when a new approach is needed.
4. Give each candidate a main idea. Choose across the six axes from `principles`, using the medium skills. Make the parts support that idea: form, behavior, construction, and upkeep must fit together. Include access, failure, recovery, and change over time where they matter.
5. Make the cheapest prototype that can expose the most consequential uncertainty. A sketch can test arrangement; it cannot prove timing, physical grip, or structural strength. Match the prototype's fidelity to the question, then try it in the conditions that matter. Use `principles-measure` to define and collect quantitative evidence.
6. Compare the candidates against the same need, constraints, and named criteria. Use the comparison procedure in `principles-measure` for quantitative claims, and named criteria with direct observation for experiential judgments. Show the trade-offs and evidence. Choose a coherent whole rather than combining incompatible favorite parts. If none serves the need, reopen the framing or constraints with their owner.
7. Leave what another maker needs to build and check the design: a model, sketch, sample, or prototype. Record why the important choices were made and how to test unresolved assumptions. Settle the interfaces with `principles-integrate`. Keep the chosen intent and criteria through implementation and refinement.

## Principles

These are heuristics. Scale exploration and documentation to what a wrong choice would cost.

| Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Design for the actual situation | An elegant answer to a need nobody has | What evidence shows who needs it, where they meet it, and what they must do? |
| Let constraints shape the approach | An attractive concept patched late to fit reality | Does the candidate meet every hard constraint before its preferences are compared? |
| Explore choices that could change the answer | One early idea defended by cosmetic variants, or needless alternatives after the answer is clear | Which unresolved decision could favor a different approach, and what would settle it? |
| Give the whole one main idea | Parts that compete for attention or require conflicting behavior | Can each consequential choice be traced to the intent, and do the parts work together in use? |
| Test the property the decision depends on | A persuasive picture taken as proof of use or construction | Could this prototype fail on the property being claimed, under the relevant conditions? |
| Preserve the reasons for the choice | Later changes undo a trade-off nobody remembers | Can another maker tell what is fixed, what is provisional, and why? |
| Settle costly-to-reverse choices first: a stored data shape, a public promise, or a cut part before a color | A cheap choice is debated while a costly one is made by default | Which open decisions are costly to undo, and are they settled first, on evidence? |
| When one idea serves several situations, such as devices, sizes, or audiences, state what stays the same and what adapts to each, and test each situation alone | A shrunk copy that fits none, or separate designs that share nothing | For each situation, which choices changed, and why? |

Pairs with: before `principles-method`, `principles-craft`, `principles-represent`; after `principles-measure`, `principles-synthesize`, `principles-integrate`, `principles-refine`, `principles-verify`.
