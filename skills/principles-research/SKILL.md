# Research

In one line: State the question and what counts as enough evidence, then trace every answer to its sources.

Research gathers and connects evidence to answer a question. Start by stating the question, who needs the answer, and what would count as enough evidence.

Use this skill for the path from question to sources to findings. Use `principles-ground` for kinds of claim, competing explanations, inference, and whether more information is worth gathering. Use `principles-measure` when collecting or comparing measurements. A research request authorizes investigation within its scope; preserve the user's limits on experiments, access, spending, and publication.

## Steps

1. Frame the question. State the decision or understanding it should support, the reader, scope, and required form of the answer. Break a broad topic into questions that can receive separate answers. Name what would change the answer. Match the effort to the consequences with `principles-method`.
2. Plan the search. Identify the evidence each question needs, likely sources, and inclusion limits such as population, setting, period, or version. Set a time or effort limit and a stopping condition. For exploratory work, start with a broad question and record how it changes. For a systematic review, define the search and selection method before screening and record later changes.
3. Find and read sources. Start with available project records, original work, and sources responsible for the claim. Use summaries and search results to locate the underlying evidence. Use an available search, library, or repository tool; direct reading and a manual source log also work. If access fails, record the gap and its effect on the answer.
4. Record support as you read. For each material claim, keep the source identity, a stable locator, the supporting passage or data location, and relevant date or version. Note the conditions, method, limits, and conflicts of interest. Date access to changing sources. Preserve enough search terms and selection reasons for someone to retrace the important choices. Save notes incrementally in the project's usual place, or a task-specific file when none exists.
5. Challenge the emerging answer. Search for contrary findings, missing cases, and alternative terms. Trace repeated claims to their origin before treating them as independent support. Resolve disagreements by comparing definitions, methods, populations, dates, and conditions. Leave an unresolved conflict visible. Use `principles-ground` to assess what follows from the evidence.
6. Synthesize around the question. State what the sources support, how they fit together, and where they disagree. Distinguish findings from your inference or proposed action. Compare options against the same relevant criteria. Keep an unknown value visible instead of scoring it as a failure. When a gap needs an experiment, propose the smallest discriminating check; run it only within the authorized scope.
7. Stop and report. Apply the stopping rule from `principles-ground` and the agreed effort limit. At the limit, report a partial answer and the remaining uncertainty. Check each material claim against the source you read, place its citation beside it, and verify locators. Lead with the answer or the reason it remains open. Keep the sources, scope, search limits, and next useful check with the findings. Use `principles-verify` before handoff.

## Principles

| Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Let the question choose the evidence | A collection of interesting sources never answers the request | Which question does each finding answer, and what evidence would answer the missing part? |
| Match authority to the claim | An official statement or a frequently cited paper is treated as proof of something it did not establish | Does the source support this exact claim under these conditions? A first-party source establishes what its author states; performance or causal claims need suitable evidence |
| Keep the path to the evidence | A citation points to a source that was not read or does not support the wording | Can another reader find the supporting passage, data, or code and reproduce the inference? Mark secondhand evidence and access limits |
| Count independent evidence | Many pages repeat one unsupported claim | Trace the material claims to their original evidence. How many independent observations remain? |
| Search beyond the favored answer | Search terms and selection silently exclude conflicting results | Try terms and sources that could overturn the answer. State the strongest contrary evidence and what you excluded |
| Carry conditions into the synthesis | Results from different settings appear to agree or contradict when they answer different questions | Compare definitions, methods, population, period, and version before combining findings |
| Bound claims about absence | An unsuccessful search becomes proof that a thing does not exist | Say where and how you searched. What coverage would justify the stronger claim? A bounded search supports only a bounded absence claim |
| Stop with an honest result | More reading replaces a conclusion, or an effort limit becomes a false claim of completeness | Which material question remains open, what could change the answer, and why did the search stop? |
| What people did outranks what they say they would do | Stated interest counted as demand | Is each claim about people backed by observed action, and by how many distinct people? |

## Completion

The reader receives an answer, or a bounded unresolved result, with support they can inspect. The report states its scope, material gaps, conflicting evidence, and what could change the conclusion. Its search record supports retracing the work; a large source count alone establishes neither coverage nor truth.
