# Touch

In one line: Shape where the body meets the thing, and check contact, weight, temperature, and edges in the hand.

When designing for touch, shape the points where a body meets an object or a space. Check contact, temperature, weight, and edges in the hand, and movement through a room.

## Laws of the medium

- The body is the instrument. Measure with it before you trust a drawing.
- Design for the ends of the range. The middle takes care of itself.
- Contact area and force set pressure. Spread the load where the body rests.
- Feel is heat flow, force, and friction. Name the mechanism before you choose the material.
- A control found by hand and confirmed by feel needs no look.
- Repetition is the real test. A small cost per use is a large cost per year.
- Wear is a design input. Decide what the object looks and feels like after ten thousand touches.

## Defaults to replace

Dimensions taken from the average person, sharp edges left from the machining, uniform finish over the whole product, metal where skin rests, all controls the same size and shape, a detent unrelated to the action, a glove-free test, a seated and rested test user, a flat constant ceiling light, hard parallel walls, and a threshold nobody tested with wheels.

## Form

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body range | Design to a range of bodies, not an average. Reach, grip, and sight go to the small end of the range; clearance, door width, and seat room go to the large end | Sized to the mean, so both ends of the range are shut out | State the smallest and largest user. Test the part with each |
| Edges and radii | Contact area sets pressure for a given force. A sharp edge concentrates the load and cuts or bruises; a generous radius spreads it | Hard edges where skin, palm, or wrist rests | Press a fingertip along every edge a body meets. Any line of pressure is a fault |
| Grip | A grip works when the hand wraps the form and the thumb opposes the fingers. Diameter must let the fingers close without gaps or overlap, and a power grip needs a larger diameter than a precision grip | One diameter for both heavy force and fine control | Hold it as used. Do the fingers close, and does the load sit on the palm, not a point? |
| Reach | Reach is a sphere around the shoulder, smaller seated than standing and smaller still when force is needed | A frequent control at the edge of reach | Place the smallest user in the real posture. Mark what lies inside easy reach |
| Clearance | A moving or posed body sweeps a volume. Leave room for the body plus its clothing, its tools, and a margin | Gaps sized to a bare, still body | Sweep a full-size model through the motion, with gloves and a load |
| Scale to the body | Dimensions that meet the body come from the body: elbow height, hand breadth, eye height, stride | Dimensions copied from the last product | List each dimension that meets the body and name the body measure behind it |

## Material

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermal feel | Skin senses its own temperature, set by how fast heat leaves it. A material that conducts heat away quickly, with a high thermal effusivity (the square root of conductivity times density times specific heat), feels cold even at room temperature. A low-effusivity material such as wood or foam feels warm at the same temperature | Metal on a surface held in cold air, or in a hot sun, with no thought for how it feels | Touch it at the temperature of use. Does it feel as intended, and is it safe to hold at the extremes? |
| Hardness | Hardness sets whether the surface dents, scratches, or stays crisp, and whether a hard knock hurts the body | Hard surfaces at head or knee height | Where can a body strike it? Is the surface forgiving there? |
| Friction | Grip needs friction; friction depends on the pair of surfaces, moisture, oil, and the contact pressure. Wet, oily, or gloved hands change it | A smooth finish on a part held with wet hands | Test the grip dry, wet, oily, and gloved |
| Texture | Skin reads texture at a fine scale, so surface pattern guides the hand to grip, to a control, or to an edge | Uniform texture everywhere, so the hand cannot find anything | Close your eyes. Can the hand tell the controls from the body? |
| Weight and density | Weight is felt as quality, but only to the point where it tires. Mass should sit where the hand and the balance point can carry it | Heavy at the end of a lever arm | Hold it at arm's length for a minute. Where does the weight sit? |
| Compliance | A soft layer spreads pressure over contact area and raises comfort, but a layer too soft bottoms out and gives no support | Foam that compresses to the base, or a rigid surface under long contact | Press with a body part for a long time. Does the pressure stay even? |

Honest materials: a surface should feel like what it is made of, and a hollow part should not be sold as solid.

## Energy

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Force to operate | Required force sets who can use it and how often. The weakest user and the thousandth repeat set the limit, not the first try | Force tuned to a strong user on a fresh part | Measure force with a gauge. Test with the weakest intended user |
| Resistance | Resistance tells the hand it is working, and the right amount tells it how far to go | No resistance, so the hand overshoots; or high resistance for a light job | Does resistance rise as the action nears its end? |
| Travel and detents | Travel is the distance the control moves; a detent is a felt step. A click must come at the point of action, so the hand knows without looking | A control that acts before or after its click, or has no click | Press slowly with eyes closed. Does the feel mark exactly when it acts? |
| Vibration and haptics | Skin senses vibration by frequency, with the greatest sensitivity in a middle band; a pulse should be strong enough to notice and short enough not to numb. Haptics replace sight when the eyes are busy | Constant buzz that the body ignores, or feedback that fires on every event | Is each pulse distinct from background vibration? Does it mean one thing? |
| Effort over a day | Small loads repeated all day add up. Static posture and a held grip tire muscle even at low force | Tested for a minute, used for a shift | Run the real task for the real duration. Where does the body complain first? |

## Observer

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posture | The body at work is bent, reaching, and twisted, not standing straight. Joints work best near the middle of their range | Design for a neutral pose the user never holds | Photograph the real posture. Is any joint near its limit? |
| Body size range | People vary widely in every dimension, and the dimensions vary independently. Use percentile ranges of the intended population, and one person is rarely average in all dimensions | Fit judged on a single average model | Choose the percentile range to cover. Test at both ends, in each dimension |
| Strength range | Strength differs by person, by age, by direction of push, and by hand versus whole body | A force that assumes a strong adult | Use the lower end of the strength range. Can that user do it? |
| Ability and disability | Permanent, temporary, and situational limits overlap: one arm in a cast, one holding a child, one carrying a bag. A solution for the narrower case serves the wider one | Features that need two hands, fine grip, or sharp vision | Operate it one-handed, with low vision, and with reduced grip |
| Gloves | Gloves thicken fingers, cut touch, and cut grip, so controls and gaps must work in them where gloves are used | Small controls close together, tested bare handed | Test with the real gloves, wet or cold if they are |
| Fatigue | A tired body reaches less, grips weaker, and makes more errors | Fit and force set for the fresh user | Test at the end of the work, not the start |
| The user who cannot see it | Touch and shape can carry meaning when sight is off, lost, or busy. A control found by hand should be distinct by shape, location, or texture | Controls the same to the hand, found only by looking | Operate it in the dark. Can each control be told apart by hand? |

## Composition

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control layout | Place by frequency, then by sequence: the most used nearest the easy reach, controls used in order laid out in that order, controls of one function grouped | Layout by internal wiring or by the drawing's neatness | List controls by use frequency. Does proximity follow it? |
| Separation | Controls that do different things, or whose mistaken use costs a lot, sit apart or differ in shape, resistance, or guard | Dangerous and harmless controls side by side and alike | For each control, what happens if the neighbor is hit? |
| Circulation | People move along paths, and a path is the shortest line between what they want. Clear paths along those lines; obstacles in them cause detours or collisions | Furniture or walls across the natural line, or paths that cross the busiest ones | Trace a person's route for each common task. Where do paths cross? |
| Zones | A space divides by activity, noise, and privacy, with a transition between zones. Related activities adjoin and conflicting ones separate | Everything in one open space, or adjacency by accident | Draw the zones. Which neighbors conflict? |
| Thresholds | A threshold marks a change of state. It should be noticeable to the body through a step, a change in light, a narrowing, or a change in floor, and safe for every user | A silent change of level, or a threshold nobody can cross with a cart or chair | Cross it blind, with a cart, a stick, and a chair |

## Time

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| First touch versus the thousandth | The first contact sets the opinion; the thousandth sets the use. A feel that charms once may irritate after a month | Judged only on first handling | Use it daily for a month before judging |
| Wear | Surfaces wear where hands, feet, and bodies touch most. Materials that wear in patterns that look good or stay functional age well | A finish that polishes slick, flakes, or reveals a cheap layer under it | Which areas will be touched most? What shows after a year of that? |
| Patina | Patina is wear that adds character, as a mellowed edge or a darkened handle. It needs a material that wears evenly | A coating that scars and cannot recover | Rub the finish hard. Does it improve, or fail? |
| Aging of materials | Foams settle, rubbers harden and crack, polymers yellow and embrittle, adhesives creep, and metals oxidize at rates set by heat, light, and chemistry | Comfort and grip specified at new condition only | Age a sample by the damage that matters: heat, light, sweat, cleaning agents |
| Maintenance and repair | A thing used for years must be cleaned, tightened, and mended by its owner. Worn parts that are touched most should be replaceable | A sealed part whose touched surface cannot be renewed | Which parts wear out first? Can the owner replace them? |

## Space

| Leaf | Principle | Failure | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light as felt | Daylight changes in direction, color, and level through the day, and the body uses these cues to keep its rhythm. Light from the side models the room; glare from a bright source against a dark ground tires the eye | One flat, constant light level, or a bright window as the only light | Visit at three times of day. Is the light changing, and is any source in the eye? |
| Sound as felt | Hard parallel surfaces reflect sound and extend reverberation; soft and irregular surfaces absorb and scatter it. Intelligibility and calm both depend on reverberation time | A hard-walled room where speech must be clear | Clap in the room. How long does the sound hang? |
| Air as felt | Comfort rests on air temperature, radiant temperature of surfaces, air speed, humidity, and fresh air. A body can lose heat by radiation to a cold wall even when the air is warm | Air temperature alone on the thermostat | Measure surface temperatures and air speed. Is the body losing heat unevenly? |
| Proportion | Rooms read by the ratio of height to width and length, and by the human scale in them. A tall narrow room is formal; a low wide room is intimate | Dimensions from the structure grid alone | Stand in it. Does the ratio fit the use? |
| Refuge and prospect | People settle where they can see without being seen: a back to a wall or an alcove, with a view out. The mix of a protected place and an open view is felt as comfort | Seats in the open, or a hidden corner with no view | For each seat, what is behind it, and what can be seen from it? |
